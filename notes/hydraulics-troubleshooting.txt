HYDRAULICS INTERMITTENT CUTOUT — TROUBLESHOOTING NOTES
========================================================

SYMPTOM
-------
All hydraulics (steering + 3-pt lift + remotes) drop out simultaneously
and intermittently.

Recovery: turning the steering wheel past a certain angular position
(either direction) restores hydraulic function. The trigger is
ROTATIONAL POSITION, not force — it is not a "pull hard" recovery,
it kicks in once the wheel passes a threshold angle.

Load correlation:
  - PTO running (wet clutch engaged): cutouts are frequent. During a
    bad episode the system cycled on/off roughly every ~10 seconds.
  - Driving in 2WD or 4WD without PTO: no symptoms across extended use.
  - Light steering load on its own does not appear to trigger it.

The 10-ish-second cycling is not a fixed cadence — it is irregular and
correlates with load, not a timer.

Two distinct recovery timescales observed:
  - Short (seconds to tens of seconds): turning the steering wheel past
    a threshold angle restores hydraulic function briefly during an
    active bad session. Repeats every ~10 s under PTO load.
  - Long (hours to days, possibly weeks): the entire system runs
    symptom-free for extended periods between bad sessions, then
    symptoms return without an obvious trigger.

The long fault-free intervals are the key constraint on root cause and
materially reshape the sub-cause ranking under the leading theory below.
A continuous mechanical defect (e.g. broken centering springs) cannot
spontaneously restore itself for days, so the dominant sub-cause is
something intermittent — particle, contamination, or pump-side air.


LOGGED EPISODE 2026-10-26 (Kelly telemetry) — PUMP IS DEAD-HEADED
-----------------------------------------------------------------
First dropout captured with Kelly telemetry (1 Hz, logged by the ESP32
in data_*.jsonl): logs/sync/2026.10.26/s00010-soc77, uptime ~2476-4008 s.
No earlier Kelly-bearing log (2026-07-02 onward, ~70 sessions) contains
a stall; the June episodes above predate Kelly logging.

Signature of every dropout:
  - Pump motor RPM 0 while phase current sits flat at 126-128 A (the
    controller's current limit), hall state frozen, TPS on. Battery
    draw only ~13-15 A — the power goes into the stalled windings.
  - Onset from full speed is abrupt (2600-2800 rpm at 20-30 A one
    sample, stalled the next). Two onsets also latched Kelly "Hall
    Sensor Error", one a "Locking" (locked-rotor) fault.
  - Restart attempts with the switch cycled: current ramps ~15 A/s
    from 0 to the limit and the rotor never turns.
  - Once (t=3224 s) the motor broke free and returned to ~2500 rpm with
    the switch still on.
  - Operator restored hydraulics by turning the wheel HARD RIGHT.
  - Motor temp climbed 53 -> 74 C during one 39 s stall (session max
    83 C).

Interpretation (CONFIRMED by wheel-turn recovery during a logged stall):
the motor is not cutting out on its own — it is being hydraulically
locked. The pump is pushing into a blocked path and stalls at current
limit. This upgrades the leading theory below from "restricted"
open-center passage to "fully blocked": the HSU spool hangs closed, and
since the only system relief is in the lift block downstream of the
HSU, nothing limits pump pressure except the motor stalling. The hall
error, Locking fault, and TPS dip (254 -> 249, only while stalled) are
consequences of the stall, not causes — wiring/hall checks are demoted.

Follow-ups:
  - Does the HSU (D10524330) have an integral relief valve? If so, it is
    not opening — inspect it with the spool.
  - Is recovery only to the RIGHT now? (June notes: either direction.)
    A one-sided recovery suggests the spool hangs offset to one side.
  - Motor protection: each stall puts ~127 A through stationary
    windings. Switch the pump off (or turn the wheel) promptly rather
    than letting it sit locked.


SYSTEM TOPOLOGY (per FT 25G service manual)
-------------------------------------------
Single gear pump (suction strainer in tank) feeds an open-center
hydrostatic steering unit (HSU) in series ahead of the rear lift block.

  Tank -> Strainer -> Pump -> HSU P port -> HSU T port
                                              |
                                              v
                              Lift block "P" -> Relief valve
                                              -> Control valve
                                              -> Unload valve
                                              -> PTO wet clutch feed/return
                                              -> Tank

Key consequences of this topology:
  - The HSU is upstream of everything else. If it chokes, all downstream
    hydraulics (lift, remotes, PTO clutch) die WITH the steering.
  - In neutral the HSU's open-center passage passes pump flow straight
    through to the lift block.
  - In neutral the lift unload valve dumps that flow back to tank
    (open-center; no standby pressure maintained).
  - The PTO wet clutch is the highest sustained flow consumer on the
    system — it needs both holding pressure and cooling flow whenever
    PTO is engaged.
  - The lift block's pressure relief (top of lift cover, M30 cartridge,
    169 bar crack / 180-187 bar full open) protects everything
    downstream of the HSU.

Sources:
  - Service manual pp 195-204 (Steering chapter): HSU schematic,
    neutral flow, manual-pump fallback, air-purge procedure.
  - Service manual pp 295-319 (Hydraulic chapter): lift block layout,
    relief valve location and spec, troubleshooting table p. 22.
  - DOCUMENTATION.md:207-228: full hydraulic system is mechanical;
    no CAN, no sensors, no solenoids. The e-hydraulic controller has
    no CAN pins. PTO wet clutch requires pump pressure to engage
    and oil flow for cooling.


LEADING THEORY: HSU SPOOL HUNG OFF-NEUTRAL
------------------------------------------
The HSU spool is sitting in an off-neutral position when the wheel is
near center, instead of fully returning to the open-center pass-through
position. In that hung position the open-center passage that feeds the
downstream lift block is partially or fully restricted.

This single failure mode explains all four observations:

  a) Wheel-position-dependent recovery — turning the wheel mechanically
     drives the spool via the input shaft into a working porting
     position regardless of where the (presumably weak) centering
     springs would otherwise park it.

  b) Light-load operation is fine — even a restricted open-center
     passage can pass enough flow to maintain pressure when nothing
     downstream is drawing much. Driving without PTO is mostly idle
     hydraulics.

  c) PTO load makes it worse — wet clutch + cooling is the largest
     continuous flow draw on the system. A restricted passage cannot
     keep up; downstream pressure collapses.

  d) ~10 s cycling under PTO load — the HSU spool is pressure-balanced.
     With weak centering springs and varying downstream pressure, the
     spool drifts cyclically between restrictive and flowing positions,
     producing a self-sustaining oscillation (pressure rises -> spool
     pushed to restrictive -> pressure falls -> spool released -> pressure
     rises). ~10 s is plausible for the time constant of pressure rise
     and decay in this circuit.

Likely sub-causes within this theory, ranked by consistency with the
hours-to-days fault-free intervals observed between bad sessions:

  1. Particle in the spool/sleeve clearance. A 5-10 µm chip can dislodge
     under a pressure spike or thermal cycle, get shuttled around by
     flow, and re-wedge elsewhere later. Classic intermittent
     contamination signature; cleanly explains both the short-term and
     long-term recovery timescales.

     SUPPORTED. The symptom pre-existed the most recent fluid change,
     and a fair amount of FERROUS DEBRIS was found stuck to the
     drain-plug and strainer magnets at that change. The operator's
     working interpretation is that this debris is residual from
     manufacture / break-in, not from an active wear source: machining
     swarf, casting flash, and assembly leftovers that accumulate on
     magnets over the first few hundred hours of a new tractor's life
     and then taper off. This is plausible and the most benign reading.

     Whether the source is break-in residue or active wear is the key
     open question, with very different implications:
       - Break-in residue: the first fluid change removed most of it.
         Remaining suspended particles are what's still hanging the
         spool. Expected trajectory: symptoms taper as further fluid
         changes catch the remainder, with no need to open any
         component.
       - Active wear: a component is shedding metal continuously.
         Fluid changes reduce concentration but don't fix the source.
         Expected trajectory: symptoms persist or worsen until the
         worn component is identified and replaced.

     The cleanest disambiguator is the SECOND-CHANGE COMPARISON:
     change fluid, run a defined duty cycle (5-10 hours of normal use
     including PTO), drop the strainer and drain-plug magnets, and
     compare debris quantity to last time. Much less = break-in
     hypothesis confirmed. Same or worse = active wear, proceed to the
     wear-source suspects below.

  2. Spool/sleeve wear producing temperature/viscosity-sensitive
     stiction. A polished bore with cold thick oil can hang the spool;
     warm thin oil clears it, then the next cool-down brings it back.
     Compatible with long good periods if they correlate with warmed-up
     running and bad sessions with cold starts.

  3. Centering springs broken, distorted, or fatigued. WEAKENED by the
     long fault-free intervals: metal fatigue doesn't reverse, so a
     spring too weak today shouldn't be strong enough to center the
     spool for days at a stretch tomorrow. Not ruled out (a partially
     fractured blade could occasionally align favorably under specific
     load) but no longer the leading sub-cause.


WEAR SOURCE SUSPECTS (only if break-in hypothesis is rejected)
--------------------------------------------------------------
Only relevant if the second-change comparison shows debris quantity
holding steady or rising — i.e. the break-in / manufacturing-residue
reading above turns out to be wrong. If the second change comes back
visibly cleaner, skip this entire section.

Candidates for an active wear source, ranked by match to the PTO-load
correlation and the rate that "fair amount" implies if it is in fact
continuous shedding:

  1. PTO wet clutch wearing or slipping. Wet clutches always shed some
     metal during normal engagement, but "fair amount" suggests
     accelerated wear. A slipping clutch would:
       - generate more ferrous debris than a healthy one;
       - demand more flow / pressure to engage fully;
       - tie the metal-shedding rate to PTO use specifically.
     The PTO-load correlation in the symptom and the debris source
     could be the same underlying problem: the clutch is degrading
     under its own use, and the wear products are what's hanging the
     HSU spool. Inspect: drain the wet clutch pack and check disc
     thickness vs spec; look for glazing or burn marks on steel
     separator plates.

  2. Gear pump internal wear. The single gear pump has steel gears
     running against an aluminium or cast-iron body. Wear products are
     ferrous and fine. A worn pump also explains:
       - declining output / higher internal leakage on hot oil;
       - flow shortfalls under high downstream demand (PTO);
       - intermittent dropouts that get worse over time.
     Not directly inspectable without pulling the pump; an indirect
     test is a flow/pressure measurement at the pump outlet during a
     dropout (step 9 in the diagnostic procedure).

  3. HSU gerotor wear. The gerotor's steel rotor on a steel/cast-iron
     stator is a wear surface too. Less likely as the primary source
     here because steering power-assist still feels normal in healthy
     intervals — significant gerotor wear would degrade steering effort
     even between dropouts. Worth inspecting if the unit comes out.

  4. Wet clutch hydraulic motor or other downstream rotating part.
     Less common, but listed for completeness.

Diagnostic for narrowing this down: change fluid, run a short defined
duty cycle (e.g. 5 hours including PTO use), then drop the drain and
strainer and compare debris quantity to last time. Same or worse =
active source still feeding at the same rate. Much less = a one-off
contamination that the previous flush largely removed. The shape and
size of the debris also matters (photograph it on a magnet under good
light):
  - Fine grey powder: pump or gerotor wear.
  - Larger flakes / curls: gear pump teeth or clutch disc fragments.
  - Bronze or non-ferrous: bushings / thrust washers (different source
    category entirely).


OTHER POSSIBLE CAUSES (ranked, less likely)
-------------------------------------------
2. Air ingress on the suction side.
   - A loose suction fitting or porous hose lets air in but never drips
     oil. Causes intermittent pump aeration.
   - Fits the wheel-cycling-as-air-purge mechanism, but does not as
     cleanly explain the PTO correlation or the ~10 s cycling cadence.

3. Hydraulic pump losing prime intermittently.
   - Explicitly listed in the lift troubleshooting table (p. 22, item 2,
     "Failure to Lift Under All Conditions"). Cranking the HSU
     re-circulates fluid and can re-prime the pump.
   - Same weakness as above: doesn't naturally produce PTO-load-
     correlated cycling.

4. Low fluid level.
   - Cheapest to rule out. Manual warns it causes steering assist loss
     before anything else.

5. Sticking system relief at the lift block.
   - Could kill everything downstream, but no mechanism for wheel-
     position recovery — the lift block isn't coupled to the steering
     column.


DIAGNOSTIC PROCEDURE (do in this order)
---------------------------------------
1. Check hydraulic fluid level on the dipstick, cold, on level ground,
   lift arms fully down, all remotes retracted. Top up only to the
   upper line (not above — overfill causes its own aeration issues).

2. SELF-CENTERING / DEAD-ZONE TEST. Engine on, pump on, vehicle
   stationary. Lightly hold the wheel and release. Does it have a
   distinct neutral feel? Is there a dead zone of ±10-30° near center
   where small inputs produce nothing visible at the wheels? A vague
   neutral or a noticeable dead zone strongly suggests HSU centering
   spring failure.

3. STEERING-VS-LIFT CORRELATION DURING DROPOUT. Next time hydraulics
   cut out:
     - Does the STEERING also feel different (heavy, dead spot, vague)?
     - Or does the steering feel normal but only the lift dies?
   "Steering normal, lift dead" weakens the HSU theory (steering
   working-port circuit is fine). "Steering feels off too" strengthens
   it (the open-center pathway that feeds the lift is also the same
   passage used in steering neutral).

4. RECOVERY DURATION TEST. After recovery via wheel input, let the
   wheel sit at center, do not touch it. Time until next dropout.
     - Seconds to tens of seconds: spool hung again at neutral -
       centering springs / spool stiction.
     - Many minutes: air pocket re-accumulating - suction side leak.
     - Random / load-dependent: aligns with hung-spool + PTO theory.
     - Hours to days of complete fault-freedom between bad sessions:
       points away from continuous mechanical defects (worn springs,
       static stiction) and toward intermittent causes - particle
       wandering in the spool/sleeve clearance, conditional
       suction-side air ingress, or pump-side intermittent (Kelly
       controller, internal pump leakage). This is the single most
       informative timescale to record; it directly demotes broken
       centering springs as a candidate.

5. KELLY CONTROLLER DIAGNOSTICS. Connect to the Kelly KLS pump motor
   controller via its RS-232 diagnostic port (separate from CAN; see
   Kelly KLS user manual for pinout). Run the free "KLS User Program"
   in a Windows laptop (Wine works). During a dropout, look at:
     - Motor RPM and phase current
     - Controller and motor temperature
     - Active and historical fault codes
   Purpose here is to rule electrical OUT, not in. If motor is running
   normally with normal current during a dropout, the cutout is
   downstream of the pump — confirming the hydraulic theory and
   localizing it to the HSU / lift block / relief.
   DONE 2026-10-26 (see LOGGED EPISODE above): during a dropout the motor
   is NOT running — it is stalled at current limit (~127 A, 0 rpm),
   i.e. the pump is dead-headed by a blocked path. Electrical ruled out
   as the root cause; wheel-turn recovery confirmed hydraulic lock.

6. Inspect suction-side plumbing between reservoir and pump inlet for
   loose clamps, soft/cracked hose, weeping fittings.

7. Pull and inspect the suction strainer for metal debris. Photograph.
   Even after a fluid change, debris that was previously circulating
   may have collected here.

8. If steps 1-7 don't resolve: pull the HSU for inspection and
   cleaning (see next section).

9. Optional / harder: tee a pressure gauge into the line between the
   HSU T port and the lift block "P" inlet. During a dropout:
     - 0 bar: HSU is blocking flow OR pump is dead/cavitating.
              Cross-check with a second gauge at the pump output (P port
              of HSU) to disambiguate.
     - Pressure present but no work happening: issue is downstream of
       the HSU (relief stuck, control valve issue, etc.)


HSU REMOVAL AND INSPECTION
--------------------------
Removal is documented in steering chapter p. 4:
  1. Disconnect battery ground.
  2. Clean the hose/pipe connections (debris control).
  3. Mark and disconnect the four hoses (P, T, L, R), cap pipe ends.
  4. Slacken pipe retainer bolts on the engine side.
  5. Remove lower steering column shroud, disconnect harness.
  6. Pull four bolts on steering motor support bracket.
  7. Slacken clamp bolts on column-to-HSU coupling.
  8. Withdraw HSU from LH side of tractor.
Plan ~2 hours. Wash the unit thoroughly before opening.

Internal disassembly is NOT factory-documented in this manual but is
standard for HSUs of the Danfoss OSPC / Eaton Char-Lynn class
(which this almost certainly is or clones).

Typical internal layout:
  end-cap bolts (~7x) -> end cover
                      -> gerotor set (stator + rotor)
                      -> drive link (cardan shaft)
                      -> manifold plate (with check ball)
                      -> spool + sleeve (lapped matched pair)
                      -> centering springs (3 or 6 flat blades)
                      -> housing

What to inspect:
  - Centering springs: distortion, fatigue, broken blades. PRIMARY
    suspect given the position-dependent recovery symptom.
  - Spool/sleeve: light axial scoring polishes; deep scoring kills the
    unit. Particles in this clearance produce intermittent sticking.
  - Gerotor teeth: chips, embedded metal, scoring at tooth tips.
  - Check ball + seat: pitting or debris would let flow leak from
    pressure side back to tank.
  - All O-rings: replace while open. Standard seal kits are cheap.

Critical gotchas during reassembly:
  - Spool and sleeve are a LAPPED MATCHED PAIR. Never separate from
    each other or swap with another unit.
  - Centering springs are direction-sensitive. Curve faces a specific
    direction relative to the spool slot. Wrong = no self-centering.
  - Drive link timing sets steering centering. Photograph orientation
    of input shaft, drive link, gerotor before disassembly.
  - End-cap bolts: torque in crisscross pattern. Look up torque for the
    actual unit; service manual does not specify.

Buy-vs-rebuild economics for the e25:
  - New HSU: probably $250-500.
  - Generic Char-Lynn / Danfoss seal & repair kit: $40-80.
  - Professional rebuild shop: $150-300 with warranty.
  If spool/gerotor turn out to be scored, replacement is required
  anyway; a failed rebuild attempt only costs time.


FLUSH / FLUID NOTES
-------------------
Reusing drained oil after household straining: NOT recommended.
Particles that matter for HSU damage are 5-10 µm; coffee filters and
shop strainers do not catch anything close to that size, so straining
just recirculates the contamination. Use fresh fluid for any flush.

Procedure if pursuing a flush:
  1. Drain into clean container. Let settle 30 min. Inspect for water
     layer at bottom, metal sediment.
  2. Pull and clean suction strainer.
  3. Refill with fresh UTF.
  4. Cycle steering lock-to-lock and lift up/down for 10-15 min.
  5. Drain again. Compare to step 1. Visibly cleaner = removed loose
     debris; identical = active debris source remains.
  6. Refill fresh and run.

Filling cautions:
  - Fill cold, on level ground, lift arms FULLY DOWN, all remotes
    retracted/disconnected. This puts maximum oil in the tank and
    minimum in the cylinders.
  - Fill to the upper line, NOT above. Overfill causes:
      * Burping out the breather as cylinders return / system warms.
      * Loss of de-aeration headspace -> foaming -> cavitation at pump
        -> symptoms that mimic the problem being chased.
      * Mild pressurization if breather is partly blocked -> seal weep.


THINGS CONSIDERED AND REJECTED
------------------------------
- CAN bus captures during a dropout. The e-hydraulic controller has no
  CAN pins; there is no telemetry on the bus related to this system.
  (See DOCUMENTATION.md:207-228.)

- Kelly controller current/thermal cycling as the primary cause. The
  ~10 s cadence under PTO load briefly suggested this, but it does not
  explain wheel-position-dependent recovery — a self-recovering
  controller fault would clear on its own internal timer regardless of
  steering input. Kelly diagnostics are retained in the procedure to
  rule electrical OUT, not as a prime suspect. (2026-10-26 logs: the
  Kelly faults seen — Hall Sensor Error, Locking — are consequences of
  the pump being hydraulically locked, not the cause.)

- Force-based stuck-spool theory ("crank hard, mechanical pressure
  spike pops it loose"). User reports recovery is position-dependent,
  not force-dependent, so the relevant variable is angular position of
  the spool, not the magnitude of any pressure transient.

- Sticking relief valve as a prime suspect. It could kill all
  downstream hydraulics, but it has no mechanism for wheel-position-
  triggered recovery and no reason to correlate with PTO load.

-----------------------

  169 bar, full open 180-187 bar. (Service manual hydraulic ch., Fig. 8
  and Fig. 19 item 4.)
- HSU (power steering motor assembly): part D10524330. Mounted on
  steering column bracket D10564510 above front axle on LH side.
- Steering cylinder: part D10537740, transverse mount on front axle.
- Pump model: not yet identified — check parts catalog if relevant.
